Whack-A-Mole Attack · harness reference

Mole Table Wiring

Ten 60 mm dome buttons carrying a 12-pixel NeoPixel ring and a Cherry D44X switch, plus two unlit start buttons, back to one Teensy 4.1. Pin assignments are mirrored from firmware/WhackAMole/Config.h.

The one rule

Power does not daisy-chain through the buttons. Only the data line does. A 12-pixel ring pulls 720 mA at full white, so chaining 5 V through the button connectors would put 7.2 A through the first one. Run a separate 5 V/GND bus and tap each button off it.

Cap pigtails

one harness per button, cap to PCB

The button board is a PCB mounted centrally under the table, and every cap reaches it directly: one six-conductor pigtail per button, terminated in a XHP-6 plug, long enough to run from its dome to the board with no intermediate joint. There is no per-cap junction and no bus to tap — ten identical harnesses, each one plugged into its own connector.

That is the whole reason the PCB is worth building. The stripboard version needed a 5 V/GND bus soldered across twenty strips with a stripped window at each of ten tap points; here the board distributes power in copper and the harness carries it.

Conductor colours

The same six colours on every cap, in the order the connector expects. These are conductor functions, not cap identities — the yellow wire is pixel data in all ten harnesses, including the ones inside the red and the green domes.

Two gauges in one harness, and the reason is mechanical both times. Pins 5 and 6 are 22 AWG because that is the only gauge inside both the spade receptacle's 22–18 AWG range and the JST contact's 22–28. Pins 1–4 are 28 AWG because nothing forces them to be thicker: a cap draws about 180 mA at the configured brightness, which is 38 mV round trip over half a metre, so the ring sees 4.96 V. Raise the brightness to full white and it is 153 mV — still 4.85 V, and still nowhere near anything a WS2812B reacts to. Thermally it is not close either: 180 mA against roughly 1.4 A for 28 AWG in free air.

One conductor from the ring pad to the connector, with no joint in between. An earlier revision ran 30 AWG inside the cap and spliced to a heavier pigtail, on the grounds that 30 AWG flexes best across the plunger travel. That is gone, and the reason it had to go is that 30 AWG is below the XH contact's 22–28 range — it could never be crimped, so the splice existed only to get back into spec. Running 28 AWG the whole way is in spec at both ends and removes four solder joints per cap: forty across the table, every one of them buried in a stem bore where it cannot be inspected.

If you are buying wire rather than using 28 AWG you already have, 26 AWG is the better pick — mid-range for the contact instead of at its thin edge, and 1.65× the copper. Either is fine. Use stranded silicone either way: at these gauges the insulation decides how well it survives the flexing, and PVC stiffens.

With no splice, the bore-plug strain relief is load-bearing

The splice used to absorb any pull on the harness. Without it, the far end of a half-metre run is a 28 AWG conductor soldered to a small pad on the ring, and every tug on the pigtail arrives at that pad. The zip tie at the bore plug stops being good practice and becomes the part that keeps the ring alive.

Cinch it on the bundle jacket, never on individual conductors — a tie pulled down onto bare 28 AWG insulation is a slow wire stripper. Leave a 15 mm service loop on the ring side of the tie, so the plunger travel is taken up by slack and not by the solder joints.

Pins 1–4 and pins 5–6 need different crimp nests

28 AWG and 22 AWG sit at opposite ends of what an SXH-001T-P0.6 accepts, and on an SN-28B those are two different nests. Crimp the four 28s, change nest, then the two 22s. The 22 nest closed onto a 28 AWG conductor gives a joint that passes a gentle tug today and lets go in six months — tug-test every contact before it goes into the housing, while it can still be cut off and redone.

Where the series resistors go

A WS2812 chain wants a series resistor of a few hundred ohms at each data input. All ten live inside the caps, at the receiving ring's DI pad — one per cap, no exceptions. Nothing on the controller board sits in the pixel path but wire.

Nine of them could never have gone anywhere else. The data for hops 2–10 leaves one cap's ring DO, crosses the PCB on DATA_J1_J2 and its siblings, and enters the next cap's DI — the controller board is not in that path at any point, so a resistor there would have no electrical relationship to the signal.

The tenth is a choice. The hop into cap 1 is the only one that passes through the controller board, so its resistor could sit on the stripboard instead, in series between the shifter's 1Y and ribbon position 5 — and did, in an earlier revision of this document. Moving it into the cap makes all ten caps electrically identical, so any finished cap plugs into any of the ten connectors. A spare is a spare, and there is no "cap 1 is the special one" exception to remember at two in the morning. It also matches Adafruit's own instruction to put the resistor "at the end of the wire closest to the NeoPixel(s), not the microcontroller".

Fit them as part of the ring wiring: one leg into the DI pad, the yellow 28 AWG onto the other leg, heatshrink over the body. That adds no joints — the wire was going onto that pad regardless, the resistor just lands in the middle of it. Keep the leg lengths short and let the bore plug support the body.

These are insurance, not a requirement

A pixel chain is not a passive bus — every WS2812 re-clocks and re-drives the signal, so the metre-long hops this star topology creates are driven by an active repeater at each end and will usually work bare. The reason to fit them during assembly is that retrofitting one means opening a cap. If you would rather not, build the chain, run LED WALK, and add a resistor only to a cap that sparkles or shows the wrong colour.

Length

One measurement decides the wire order, and it is the only number here that depends on your table rather than on the parts. Measure from the farthest cap's stem bore to where the PCB will sit, follow the route the harness will actually take — down the leg, along a rail, not the straight line — then add 15 % for dressing and 80 mm of service loop at the board so a connector can be unplugged without tension on it.

Cut all ten to that same length. Harnesses that differ per position cannot be swapped when one fails, and the saved wire is worth less than the ability to move a cap. For a board at the centre of a two-player table, expect 400–600 mm each, so budget roughly 6 m of each of the four 28 AWG ring colours and 7 m of each of the two 22 AWG switch colours — the switch pair serves the two start buttons as well. Order 30 ft a colour and stop thinking about it.

Start buttons

Two conductors, no ring, no power: XHP-2 into J11 and J12. White to the NO tab and green to COM, both 22 AWG — the same two colours and the same gauge as pins 5 and 6 of a cap harness, because it is the same circuit. Nothing on the start buttons is lit.

Power distribution

one supply, four loads

All four loads run from the single 5 V contract. What matters is that they are fed as a star from one point, not chained one board to the next. The rings switch current fast; share a thin feed with them and those spikes sag the rail far enough to reset a microcontroller, which presents as random crashes rather than as a power problem.

Both microcontrollers need work before they take bus power

Teensy 4.1 — cut the VUSB/VIN pads. They are bridged from the factory, so feeding VIN from the bus while USB is plugged into a computer back-feeds the bank into the computer's USB port. This is not optional here: debug mode reports over USB serial, so both will routinely be connected. Cut the two rectangular pads on the underside and confirm with a meter that they are open. It is reversible with solder — and note that afterwards USB no longer powers the board, so it needs the bus even to program.

Matrix Portal S3 — its screw terminals are outputs, not inputs. They are fed from its USB-C port and exist to pass power out to the panel. Adafruit discourages back-feeding them because plugging USB in at the same time can damage the board. Power it through its USB-C instead, from a pigtail carrying 5 V and ground off the bus; the board has its own CC pulldowns, so a dumb source is fine. Unplug that pigtail when connecting a computer. Feed the panel straight from the bus, not through the Portal.

What it looks like when you get this wrong: the panel lights red, and only red — at every colour you ask for and every brightness, usually with banding that crawls as the scan runs. Red dice forward at about 1.8 V while green and blue need nearer 3.0 V, so a rail dragged down by the long thin path through the Portal's USB-C connector and PCB traces can still light the red die and nothing else. The tell is that it does not change with brightness. A supply that merely sags under load renders a dim image correctly and only breaks near full white; one that is low all the time is red from the very first pixel. Feeding the panel straight from the star fixes it outright.

That pigtail hangs off the star, not off a second port on the bank. A bank shares one power budget across its ports, so a second cord buys no headroom — and it leaves the Portal's ground meeting the controller's only inside the bank, which drags the return path of the Serial1 display link outside the star. One supply, one star, four loads.

Adafruit publishes no continuous current rating for the HUSB238 breakout — only that the chip negotiates up to 5 A. Nearly 2.5 A through a small board and a 2-pin terminal block is real current, so check it for warmth on the LED ALL screen. If it runs hot, take VBUS through a separate high-current path and leave the HUSB238 doing nothing but the negotiation.

Two things to verify, not assume

The 3 A contract has to be real. A USB-C source only offers 3 A if the sink asks properly over the CC lines. A plain breakout without the 5.1 kΩ pulldowns gets no VBUS at all on a C-to-C cable; a generic one may only be offered 500 mA. Use a trigger board configured for 5 V, and a C-to-C cable — a USB-A-to-C cable has no CC lines and cannot carry a PD contract.

The bank has to stay awake. Power banks shut down below roughly 50–200 mA, and attract mode is the quietest the table ever gets. An active PD contract usually prevents it, but it varies by model — leave the table idling for half an hour before an event, not during one.

Then put a meter on the 5 V rail on the LED ALL screen, which is the only state that reaches worst case.

Why there is no converter

decision record

The table originally ran two 21700 cells in series into an XL4015 buck converter. That pack falls from 8.4 V to 6.0 V across a discharge and the XL4015 needs 8 V at its input, so for most of a session the converter was running below its own input floor. Fixing that is what started this change; it is worth recording why the fix deleted the converter instead of replacing the pack.

OptionAt 5 VAssessment
3S 21700 pack → XL4015≈ 4.4 AFixes the input floor and nothing else. Still loose cells, still a balance charger, still a pack that can be shorted under a table kids are hitting.
Power bank → 9 V PD → XL4015≈ 3.5 AWorks, and leaves the most room. Costs a trigger, a converter, a Schottky, and ~1.5 W as heat.
Power bank → 5 V PD chosen3.0 AFewest parts. The ceiling is lower and fixed.

What decided it

The current budget, once it was corrected. The first version counted the matrix panel at its all-pixels-white figure of 2.0 A and stacked that against the LED ALL debug screen — two worst cases that never occur together. The panel shows scrolling text on black, nearer 0.4 A. Real worst case is 2.46 A across all four loads, and gameplay is nearer 1 A, so the converter was converting power the table did not need converted.

What it buys

  • Three fewer components in the power path: the converter, its Schottky, and the wiring between them.
  • No conversion loss. The XL4015 is non-synchronous and runs 87–93%, so roughly 1.5 W would have gone to heat at worst case.
  • The 8 V input floor stops being a constraint that can ever bite, rather than one worked around.
  • A regulated 5 V rail from the bank's own converter, which is a better one than the XL4015.

What it costs

  • A hard 15 W ceiling with no room to grow. The 9 V path would have given 20 W.
  • Audio is still to come. A MAX98357A pulls around 0.6 A on peaks. Gameplay plus audio is ~1.6 A and fine, but LED ALL at full brightness with sound playing would exceed 3 A. That collision only exists in a diagnostic state, which is why it was accepted.
  • No regulation against cable drop. Whatever the USB cable and connector lose is lost from the rail, rather than being corrected by a converter downstream of it.

How to reverse it

Change the trigger's configuration resistor from 5 V to 9 V and put the XL4015 back between it and the bus. One resistor and one board — which is the reason this was a comfortable decision to make early, before any of it is soldered down.

The start buttons are switch-only

They are plain arcade buttons with no lamp, so they contribute a switch and nothing else: two conductors, no ring, and no place in the pixel chain. kPixelCount is kButtonsPerPlayer × 2 × kPixelsPerButton — 120, not 144. Allocating pixels for them would shift nothing electrically but would leave the chain expecting two rings that are not there.

Two boards

controller and breakout

Everything does not fit on one 73 × 100 mm stripboard. The Teensy alone eats 24 of the 39 strip rows, and ten six-pin headers plus their mating plugs consume both side margins with nothing left for the level shifter, the capacitors or the power entry. Splitting it also puts the ring current where it belongs — on a board the microcontroller is not sitting on.

Controller board

73 × 100 mm stripboard, strips running horizontally, Teensy socketed on female headers so it can come out. Positions below are indicative — check them against your own board before cutting anything.

Cut list — do all of it before a single component goes on

Teensy centreline, rows 11–34. 24 cuts between columns 11 and 17. Without them every strip shorts a left-row pin to the right-row pin opposite it.

74AHCT125 centreline, rows 4–10. 7 cuts between columns 7 and 10. Same rule — every DIP needs it. Here the shorts would be 1OE↔VCC (which disables the one gate you are using), 1A↔4OE, and GND↔3Y.

Rows 22 and 25, between columns 6 and 7. Two cuts. These sever the ribbon header’s strips from the Teensy’s pin 10 (reserved for audio) and its 3.3 V pin, freeing those two header positions for the ribbon’s ground and pixel data. Row 25 is the one that matters: without it the pixel data line is shorted to 3.3 V.

That is 33 cuts total. Check each with a meter before soldering — a missed cut under a soldered-down Teensy is a bad afternoon.

What the strips do for free

Twelve switch signals need no wiring at all. The ribbon header in column 3 spans rows 21–34, and on twelve of those rows the strip runs uninterrupted from the header pin straight to the Teensy pin — the strip is the wire. That is the whole reason the pin assignment is what it is.

Which way round the capacitors go

The 0.1 µF is ceramic and has no polarity — it goes in either way. Only the electrolytics care: the 100 µF here and the 1000 µF on the button board.

On a radial electrolytic the stripe down the can marks the cathode, the negative lead, and the anode is the longer lead on an untrimmed part. So on this board the striped side goes to row 2 (GND) and the long lead to row 1 (+5 V). Backwards, an aluminium electrolytic on 5 V will heat, vent and sometimes burst — it is one of the few mistakes on this board that makes a mess rather than just not working.

Check lead pitch before you commit a hole: radial electrolytics are usually 2.5 mm, which spans one row, but ceramics of this size are often 5.08 mm, which spans two. Leads bend — just know which you have so the cap lands on rows 1 and 2 and not rows 1 and 3.

Row 7 has a pin on each column, and they are opposites

2OE is at row 7, column 7 and goes to +5 V. 4Y is at row 7, column 10 and must stay unconnected — it is a disabled output. They sit on the same strip row either side of the DIP's centreline cut, so "row 7" on its own is ambiguous and tying the wrong one to 5 V puts a rail on an output pin.

The same shape applies at row 4: 1OE (col 7) goes to ground while VCC (col 10) goes to 5 V. Always carry the column with the row.

Outputs 2Y (r9 c7), 3Y (r10 c10) and 4Y (r7 c10) stay genuinely unconnected — that is what tri-state disabled means. Jumpers 8 and 9 can each be a single wire touching its several points rather than separate links, but note that 9 spans both columns of the DIP and so crosses its centreline cut; that is fine for a wire on the component side.

Fabricated, not cut: 85 × 56 mm, two layers, with GND_LED poured on both and every track at 1.0 mm. J1 to J10 are in pixel-chain order, which is not a tidy physical order. The chain runs P1 blue, red, white, green, yellow, then P2 in the same sequence, and the two rows of connectors on the board pair the players for physical tidiness only. Plug by the silkscreen reference, never by position.

The screw terminal is deliberately not a JST. J11, J12 and J14 were all 2-pin B2B-XH-A in the first revision, which meant the power lead physically fitted both start-button sockets — and landing +5 V on a start socket puts it onto a Teensy GPIO through the switch line. A screw terminal cannot accept an XH plug at all, so that mistake stopped being possible rather than merely unlikely. It also takes the 18 AWG feed directly, with no transition.

The mounting holes are unplated on purpose. Plated holes would bond GND_LED to whatever the board bolts to at four points, which is exactly the many-paths-to-ground that NT1 exists to prevent. The screws are structure, not a conductor. Use nylon standoffs if the frame is metal and you want to stop even thinking about it.

The 14-pin JST will not fit the controller's stripboard — the cable is asymmetric

JST-XH is 2.5 mm pitch and stripboard is 2.54 mm. Over the thirteen gaps of a 14-pin connector that accumulates 0.52 mm; centre it and the end pins still sit 0.26 mm out, against roughly 0.18 mm of clearance in a 1.0 mm hole. It does not seat. The six-pin version works for the same reason it is marginal — five gaps is 0.20 mm, or ±0.10 mm centred.

So the board-to-board cable has a different connector on each end: XHP-14 into J13 at the PCB, and a 2.54 mm 1×14 socket onto the pin header at the controller. That also means it cannot be IDC ribbon — XH needs individual crimped contacts, so this is a hand-crimped harness of fourteen conductors. Build it once, carefully, and label both ends.

Do not solve this by reaming the stripboard holes. You will tear copper off the strips the connector is supposed to be landing on.

Button board — stripboard alternative

The original, kept because it needs no fabrication and no two-week lead time. If you are building the PCB above, skip to Wire gauges — everything from here to there is stripboard-only, and the cut list and jumper tables do not apply to the PCB.

One dimension forces this layout. A B6B-XH-A is 5.75 mm deep — 2.3 strip rows — so connectors cannot sit on adjacent rows; they need a three-row pitch. Twelve in one column wants 36 rows against the 35 available, so it has to be two columns. That also kills the tidy scheme the controller board uses, where each signal shares a strip with its Teensy pin: the ribbon's one-row pitch cannot line up with a three-row connector pitch. Every switch signal needs a jumper here.

The chain order is not the board order

Pixels run P1 blue, red, white, green, yellow, then P2 in the same sequence — down the left column and then down the right, not left-to-right across each row. The rows pair the two players for physical tidiness and the chain ignores that pairing completely. Follow the pixel index, not the layout.

Three things stripboard will do to you

The cut under the Teensy. Strips are horizontal and the Teensy mounts vertically, so every strip crossing it shorts that row's left pin to its right pin. Cut all 24 rows between the pin rows before anything else goes on the board.

Strip current, on the button board only. Stripboard copper is roughly 35 µm × 2 mm — about 29 AWG. It carries the ring current without burning, but reinforce the two bus runs there with solid wire anyway. The controller board needs none of this: its rails feed only the level shifter and its decoupling, which is tens of milliamps.

JST-XH is 2.5 mm pitch; stripboard is 2.54 mm. Over six pins that is 0.2 mm of drift against about 0.18 mm of hole clearance. It goes in, but start each connector centred so the error splits both ways rather than accumulating toward one end.

Bring-up

test before you trust

Stages 1 to 4 are the controller board, and every test in them is done with nothing in the sockets — no Teensy, no 74AHCT125. Installed parts add their own paths (the Teensy's protection diodes especially) and turn a clear reading into a confusing one. Put them in last. The two PCB stages after them cover the button board, which has no sockets and nothing hidden.

Rows 1 and 2 cannot read open — the capacitors are across them

The 100 µF and the 0.1 µF both sit directly between the two rails, and a continuity tester pushes DC into them. An empty capacitor is a short until it charges. With a typical meter — roughly 1 mA of test current and a beep threshold of a few tens of ohms — 100 µF leaves that window in about 5 ms. So the honest expectation is a click or a chirp: not silence, and not a held tone. The button board's 1000 µF takes ten times as long, around 50 ms, which is why the chirp is easier to hear over there.

Use the resistance range instead of the beeper and the ambiguity disappears. Short the 100 µF's legs with a screwdriver to discharge it first, then probe. The reading should climb from near zero and keep climbing, settling high. A reading that parks at a stable value and stays is the fault, and the value says which kind: under 1 Ω is copper to copper, a few to tens of ohms is a short or a reversed electrolytic, and a stable kΩ is a path through a chip left in its socket.

If it holds, look in this order. The gap between rows 1 and 2, which are adjacent strips about 0.5 mm apart — walk the full length. Then rows 11 and 12 at column 17 and right, also adjacent, where the 22 AWG feed lands and where any rework on the power wires has been: a single escaped strand of stranded wire reaching the next strip is the commonest cause of this exact symptom. Then the 100 µF's polarity — striped lead to row 2. Then pull both chips: the Teensy's VIN is on row 11, which jumper 1 ties to row 1, so its regulator and bulk caps are on your rails too.

The definitive test is to lift one leg of the 100 µF. With that leg up and both sockets empty, rows 1 and 2 must read genuinely open. If they still do not, it is copper, and copper is findable.

Stage 3 — power, still no chips

  • Apply 5 V. Current draw should be essentially zero — nothing is installed. Anything above a few milliamps means a short you have not found.
  • Socket pin 14 (r4 c10) reads 5 V; socket pin 7 (r10 c7) reads 0 V. Probe the socket itself, not the strip, so the socket's own solder joints are in the measurement.
  • Teensy VIN position (r11 c17) reads 5 V; GND position (r12 c17) reads 0 V.
  • Confirm the Teensy's VUSB/VIN pads are cut — meter across them off-board, before it goes in. If they are still joined, plugging in USB back-feeds the bank into your computer.
  • Once they are cut, USB carries data only. The Teensy needs 5 V on VIN to run at all, including to be programmed — an unpowered one never enumerates, so it is simply absent from arduino-cli board list and looks unplugged. Power the board first, then connect USB.

Stage 4 — chips in

Power off. Check the socket's pin-1 end matches the chip's notch. Insert the 125 and the Teensy, re-check row 1 to row 2 the same way as in Stage 2 — chirp then climbing, never a held tone, and now with the Teensy's own bulk capacitance added to the chirp — then power up. Steady draw should be around 100 mA — the Teensy alone. A jump means a part is in backwards.

PCB Stage 2 — 5 V in, still nothing plugged in

Feed the screw terminal from the star and leave every connector empty. Steady draw should be essentially zero — C1's leakage, microamps. Anything measurable means a short the continuity checks missed, so pull power rather than looking for it live.

Then meter 5 V between pin 1 and pin 3 at all ten cap connectors. All ten within a few millivolts of each other and of the terminal; the board is a plane and a 1.0 mm track, so there is nothing here to cause a drop. One connector reading low is a partial joint that passed continuity because a meter's test current is a thousandth of what a ring draws.

Nothing on this board gets warm. If anything does, stop.

On the bench, a USB-A power pigtail is enough — and only on the bench

Before the HUSB238 and the star exist, the easiest 5 V for this stage is a moulded USB-A male pigtail with 20 AWG tinned leads — the sort sold for LED strip — straight into J14. Draw here is C1's leakage, so cable quality is irrelevant and every reading will be honest.

It cannot stay for the build. A sacrificed charge cable is usually 28 AWG: at 0.2128 Ω/m, 300 mm of it drops 231 mV round trip at the 1.81 A of LED ALL, and a metre drops 770 mV. Copper-clad aluminium adds half again, unmarked. The Type-A contacts themselves are rated only about 1.5 A. And nothing on a USB-A port negotiates — the D+/D− signature is a port advertising to a device, so a dumb load simply gets whatever that port's limiter allows.

If you do cut a cable up: keep it under 250 mm, confirm polarity with a meter on a live charger rather than trusting the colours, cut D+/D− back and heatshrink each stub separately, and do not use the braid as ground — it is a drain wire bonded to the shell at one end. Do not tin the ends either; solder creeps under screw pressure and the joint loosens over months. Bare twisted stranded, or a crimped ferrule.

Getting into debug mode with one button

Debug entry holds both start buttons, which you do not have. The inputs are INPUT_PULLUP, so grounding a pin is a press: touch a wire from ribbon positions 8 and 14 to position 2 for two seconds and the firmware sees both start buttons held.

Then LED WALK should light your cap when it reaches P1 BLUE, and BUTTONS should name it when you press. That is the whole signal path proven — level shifter, data line, switch input — on one button.

The letter that matters is the T

Order AHCT, never AHC or HC. The T means TTL input thresholds: VIH is 2.0 V, so a 3.3 V signal reads reliably as HIGH while the chip runs from 5 V and drives a 5 V output. Without it, VIH becomes 0.7 × VCC = 3.5 V and the Teensy's 3.3 V output sits below the threshold — which fails intermittently and with temperature rather than outright, and is miserable to chase.

A JST-XH connector is three part numbers

Housing, contacts and header are ordered separately, which is most of why they are hard to find. The housing on its own is an empty plastic shell.

That adds up to 64 crimps across the harness, on small open-barrel contacts that ordinary crimpers mangle. Budget for an SN-28B or equivalent, and for an evening — with a real chance of a bad crimp hiding in the loom afterwards. Two ways around it worth pricing first: pre-crimped leads, which only need a contact on the plug end since the other end goes to the cap's internal wiring, and RC balance leads — a 5S LiPo balance connector is a 6-pin JST-XH with the wires already crimped, and they are a cheap commodity.

0.187 comes in two tab thicknesses

Width is not the whole spec. The FASTON 187 series is made for tabs of either 0.020" (0.51 mm) or 0.032" (0.81 mm), and the receptacles are not interchangeable. Cherry's R1 terminals — what the D44X has — are 0.187" × 0.020".

Fit a receptacle made for the 0.032" tab onto a 0.020" one and the spring is under-deflected: reduced contact force, and a joint that reads fine on a meter and drops out when the table gets knocked. That is the intermittent-button failure this rebuild is meant to eliminate, reintroduced at the connector. TE's application spec also notes that a receptacle once mated to a 0.032" tab may no longer mate properly with a 0.020" one, so they do not mix even as spares.

Two parts can be identical on width, gauge, insulation and colour and still be the wrong one. On DigiKey, Mating Tab Thickness is its own filter — set it to 0.020" before comparing prices. TE 2-520194-2 (A27806-ND) is the cheaper trap here: correct on every other line, built for the thicker tab.

Tie the unused inputs locally, not to the star point

Ground them to pin 7 of this chip, over the shortest path on the board — not with a wire running back to the bus. During pixel switching the ground bus carries amps of fast-changing current, and wire has inductance, so ground at the star point and ground at the chip differ by a couple hundred millivolts transiently. An input referenced to a distant ground can still bounce relative to its own chip's ground. Short and local is the point.

Tying the unused inputs to VCC instead of GND works identically; convention is GND. What matters is only that they are not left floating — CMOS inputs that float oscillate, draw current, and inject noise into the gate you are actually using.

If the pixel chain is ever split into two, one per player, gate 2 is already sitting there: wiring the socket so enabling it means moving two wires costs nothing now.

Build order

test as you go
  1. Do the two board modifications firstCut the Teensy's VUSB/VIN pads and confirm with a meter. Make the Matrix Portal's USB-C power pigtail. Both are easier on the bench than under a table, and the Teensy cut changes how you program it.
  2. Wire one cap completelyRing, switch, connector, all six conductors. Getting the first one right tells you the gauge and connector choices work before you repeat them nine times.
  3. Run it on the benchLevel shifter, one finished cap with its own resistor, one button. Flash the firmware with WAM_LIGHTS_PIXELS 1 and open the debug LED WALK screen — one ring is enough to prove the data path.
  4. Order the PCB earlyTwo to three weeks of lead time runs in parallel with everything else here. Nothing downstream of the one-cap bench test is blocked by it.
  5. Build the 14-way harness and meter it end to endFourteen crimps with a different connector on each end, against the Ribbon pinout table. Continuity-check all fourteen and confirm no two are shorted before it goes near either board — it is the one part of this build that is tedious to redo in place.
  6. Populate the PCB, power it with nothing plugged inScrew terminal, the 1000 µF, the net tie, then the connectors. Feed 5 V into J14 and confirm the rail at each of the ten cap connectors with the caps still unplugged. Ten minutes here beats finding a solder bridge with ten rings attached.
  7. Make the ten pigtails to one lengthAll identical, all interchangeable. Label each at the connector end with its cap, because the chain order is not the board order.
  8. Add caps to the chain one at a timePlug them in J1 to J10 order. Each addition is a new failure point, and LED WALK names the cap it is lighting. A break shows up immediately as everything downstream going dark.
  9. Meter the rail on LED ALLThe only screen that reaches worst-case current. Do this before the table sees players, not after.
  10. Verify every switch on BUTTONSPress all twelve, the two start buttons included, and confirm each decodes as the right name. This is what catches a swapped pair in the loom.
